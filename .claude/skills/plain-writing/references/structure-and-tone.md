# Structure, context, and tone (Wes Kao)

Sentence-level plainness is necessary but not sufficient. These principles come from Wes Kao's newsletter on executive communication. Sources are linked at the bottom.

## Concise means dense, not short

> "Being concise is about using an economy of words. It's maximizing your word 'real estate.' It's about density, not absolute length."

- A 1,500-word memo can be concise. A 150-word memo can be fluffy and meandering.
- Use as many words as necessary — no more, no less. Too few words sacrifices clarity and persuasiveness.
- Some ideas cannot be reduced further. Simplifying past that point misrepresents the idea and causes bad decisions.
- **There is such a thing as too concise.** Leaving out crucial information to hit a word count is winning the battle and losing the war.
- "Make it more concise" is not useful feedback. Name the specific passage and what it costs the reader.

Applied here: never cut a rewrite so far that it loses a fact, a caveat, or a number. Density is the target — every sentence earns its place.

## Clear thinking is the bottleneck

Advice like *bottom line up front*, *don't bury the lede*, and *cut to the chase* all assume a step nobody mentions: you must first decide what the bottom line **is**.

"Figure out your main point" is really five steps:
1. Process the information
2. Make sense of it
3. Decide what's important
4. Organize it
5. Say it

Rambling happens when you do step 5 while still on step 1 — everything comes out at equal weight with no hierarchy.

Applied here: before drafting or rewriting, answer three questions.
- What do I want out of this?
- What is the single most important thing to share?
- What action do I need the reader to take?

If the source text does not answer these, say so — that is the real problem, not the prose.

**Aim for 2–3 sentences**, not one. One sentence causes analysis paralysis chasing the perfect line. Ten sentences becomes a monologue. Two to three forces you to state the thesis and the ask.

## Main point above, context below

Kao's riff on BLUF. Separate the point from the supporting detail rather than jumbling actions, backstory, and context together.

> 🚫 [Actions, backstory, and context jumbled together]
> ✅ [Main point above, context below]

This gives the reader **agency**: they read as much or as little of the context as they need. It also means you never have to choose between "concise" and "complete."

## Minimum viable backstory — start right before you get eaten by the bear

Backstory scope creep is real: you intend to give the basics, then explain the product history, your process, and three tangents.

> What's the minimum backstory necessary to set the context, so we can spend time on the important stuff?

- Rule of thumb: backstory is 10–20% of the message, not 80%.
- Skip the play-by-play of what you did at each step. Offer it if asked.
- "When you think there's nothing left to cut, cut some more."
- Say the punchline first and you instantly read as more competent, strategic, and organized.

Backstory ≠ context. Backstory is irrelevant sidebars, preamble, and rabbit holes. Context that is relevant to the decision is part of the actual story — keep it.

> 🚫 "This happened, then this other thing, and here's what I did for that, oh and this thing but that's not important…"
> ✅ "Here's 1–3 sentences of minimum viable backstory. Here's the important thing I wanted to discuss."

## Signposting: reduce the reader's cognitive load

Signposting is using words, phrases, or structure to signal where you are taking the reader, so their brain does not spend cycles guessing. It makes long writing feel shorter and more skimmable — which raises the odds it gets read and acted on.

Three levels:
- **Memo**: headers, subheaders, paragraphs, white space
- **Paragraph**: topic sentences, bullets, numbered lists
- **Sentence**: transition words, deliberate word order

Signpost words worth keeping even though they cost words:

| Signpost | What it signals |
| --- | --- |
| For example | An example follows — skippable |
| Because | Your rationale. Persuasive on its own |
| First, second, third, next, finally | Information hierarchy in prose |
| Up until now / to date / going forward | Change. *Until now we X. Going forward we will Y.* |
| For context | Background follows — skippable, gives the reader control |
| At this point | Returns to the present, sets up the conclusion |
| And, but, therefore, so | The logic of the argument |
| This means | Implications and trade-offs |
| To be clear | Preempts the pushback you know is coming |
| As a next step | Action items |

Do not swap *but* and *therefore* — they carry different logic, and interchanging them forces the reader to guess.

**Signposting is not decoration; it exposes your underlying logic.** "Up until now we X, going forward we Y" turns a vague notion into a concrete line in the sand. If you cannot fill in the structure, your thinking is not done.

**Control the emotional subtext.** Readers should never wonder "is this good news or bad news?" Signpost it: *Good news: X.*

Rule of thumb: the simpler the idea, the less signposting it needs. The longer or more complex, the more it needs.

## Numbering your questions

If you ask several questions in one message, number them. People routinely answer only the last one; a numbered list makes the omission obvious.

## Don't outsource the follow-up

> 🚫 "Let me know what you want to know."
> ✅ "I can elaborate on a few areas: (a) the other options I looked at, (b) cheaper and more expensive options I rejected, (c) the risks I foresee and how I plan to de-risk them."

If you own the work, assert what is worth sharing. Making the reader invent good questions is lazy.

## The Robot Voice Method (tone check)

Read your draft aloud in a flat, monotone, disinterested machine voice. Your reader has no access to your face, tone, or goodwill — the words carry all of it, and they may read you in the worst possible light.

1. Read in a robot voice, as blasé as possible.
2. Note anything that lands harsh, cold, or demanding.
3. Edit those spots.
4. Repeat until the flat reading still conveys the intended emotion.

Command structures are the usual culprit, especially when you are asking a favor or asking upward.

> **Before:** Please review and share your feedback.
> **After:** Could you review and share feedback?

The "before" is fine between people with an established rapport. It reads as entitled to an acquaintance. Swapping a command for a question costs nothing.

## Give the right amount of context

Too much and the reader cannot tell what matters. Too little and they must extract it from you one follow-up at a time.

- **Remind them where you left off.** Assume they read with partial attention between meetings.
  > 🚫 "Here's the updated link."
  > ✅ "Here's the updated pitch for X customer. I incorporated your feedback and summarized the changes below. I'll ship tomorrow morning unless I hear otherwise."
- **Be specific about what you need.** Never make the reader guess whether action is required.
  > 🚫 "The new ad is updated in the Google Doc. It's published in FB, but not running."
  > ✅ "Please approve the new ad copy (screenshot below). Once you approve, I'll publish and go live on FB."
- **Label an FYI as an FYI**, and say why you sent it.
- **Show your criteria and assumptions**, not just the conclusion.
  > 🚫 "I recommend this platform."
  > ✅ "I recommend this platform because of X and Y criteria. The trade-off is A, manageable because B. I also vetted [options] but rejected them because [reason]."
- **Give a concise answer, then offer to elaborate.**

Dial context **up** when the decision is irreversible, expensive, customer-facing, or new to you. Dial it **down** when it is cheap, reversible, routine for you, and already top of mind for the reader.

## Too detailed in the wrong ways, not detailed enough in the right ways

Details are not the problem. The wrong details are.

> 🚫 "Consider extending the enrollment period. For cohort 1, your course was announced on May 13, providing 6 weeks to accept students, up until the August 1 start date. For cohort 2, you announced on October 7 and only accepted for the following 10 days."
> ✅ "Consider extending your enrollment period. For your first cohort you had 6 weeks to fill it. For your second, you had barely more than 1 week. Next time, give students more time to sign up."

Why the second wins:
- Seven numbers compete for attention in the first; the eye chases all of them and the point gets lost.
- The actual point is *you had 5x as much time the first time*. Say that.
- *Providing 6 weeks* is one word shorter than *you had 6 weeks* and harder to read. **Clarity overrides conciseness.**

Ask: what does this reader need to know, and what is secondary? Every detail adds load because the reader must sort it.

## Speak in the affirmative

Say "do this" rather than "don't do that." Positive instructions are faster to process and leave less room for interpretation. A reader told what to avoid still has to work out what to do.

## Spiky point of view

Hedged, lukewarm writing is forgettable. Where the author has a real position, state it plainly and defend it — a distinctive, well-grounded opinion is more useful to the reader than bland consensus. This applies to recommendations, design docs, and reviews: say what you actually think and why, then name the trade-off.

Never manufacture a spiky opinion the author does not hold. Sharpen a position that is already there; do not invent one.
