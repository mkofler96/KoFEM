# Grammar, spelling, and punctuation traps

Sloppy mechanics do more than look unprofessional. They change meaning, and they make readers doubt everything else in the document — *are the procedures correct? does this person know the subject?*

Proofread, then ask a second reader. A fresh pair of eyes catches what you skim past.

## Subject–verb agreement

Match the verb to the real subject, not the nearest noun.

> The list of code examples **is** included in the report.

*examples* sits next to the verb, but the subject is *list* — singular.

## Prepositional phrases

Prepositions show relationships: on, over, above, below, inside, outside, with, without, by, as, of, since, despite, except. Put the phrase as close as possible to the words it modifies.

Ambiguous:
> We received the report from the data center about issues with the inventory on August 4.

Two clear rewrites, two different meanings:
> On August 4, we received the report from the data center about issues with the inventory.
> We received the report from the data center about issues with the August 4 inventory.

When you hit an ambiguity like this in someone else's text, ask which meaning was intended rather than picking one.

## Commonly confused words

- **than** (comparison) / **then** (after which) — *The pod temperature was higher **than** normal.* Writing *then* says it cooled back down, which can trigger the wrong response.
- **it's** (it is) / **its** (possessive)
- **your** (possessive) / **you're** (you are)
- **affect** (verb, to influence) / **effect** (noun, the result)
- **i.e.** (that is, in other words) / **e.g.** (for example)
- **historic** (notable in history) / **historical** (in the past, not necessarily notable)
- **they're / there / their**
- **complement** (completes) / **compliment** (praise)
- **principle** (rule) / **principal** (chief, or head of a school)
- **ensure** (make certain) / **insure** (financially) / **assure** (reassure a person)

Spell-checkers flag misspellings, not correctly spelled words used wrongly. Read for these by eye.

## Punctuation

- Periods mark where one sentence ends and the next begins. Two independent clauses joined by only a comma is a comma splice — use a period or a semicolon.
  - Wrong: *Utility power is unavailable, the load is on generators.*
  - Right: *Utility power is unavailable. The load is on generators.*
- Use a comma after an introductory phrase: *After eating, the engineers completed the task.* Without it, the engineers eat the task.
- Use the serial (Oxford) comma: *a detailed problem, potential solutions, and a list of costs.*
- Do not put a comma between a verb and its object: *Our proposal includes, a detailed problem…* is wrong.
- Colons introduce lists and explanations; semicolons join closely related independent clauses; hyphens join compound modifiers before a noun.

If you write simple, clear sentences you will need few commas — and you will not have to remember the complicated comma rules at all.
