The stiffness of a hinge bracket

A steel hinge bracket is bolted to a wall and pulled at its eye. How much does the eye move per newton? We start from an IGES file that contains no solid at all, and finish with a stiffness we can defend — after a mesh study that shows the obvious first answer was 15 % too stiff.

The task

The part is a cast steel hinge bracket: a flat mounting plate with two bolt holes, a tapering arm, and a cylindrical eye at the far end. It is 96 mm long, 40 mm wide and 33 mm tall. The mounting plate is bolted flat against a support; a pin through the eye pulls the bracket sideways with a force Fw.

We want the stiffness of the bracket in that direction:

kw = Fw / w w is the displacement of the eye along the load direction

Assumptions: linear elastic, static, small displacement. Material is steel — E = 210,000 MPa, ν = 0.3. Everything here is in the N · mm · MPa unit system, so displacements come out in mm and stiffness in N/mm.

Follow along

Download scharnier.igs (202 kB) and open the solver — everything below runs in the browser, and nothing is uploaded anywhere.

First problem: the file contains no solid

IGES is a surface format at heart. Open this file and count what is actually inside it: 31 trimmed surfaces, a pile of NURBS curves and arcs, and not one solid entity. It is a bag of patches that happen to form a closed shape — the CAD equivalent of a paper model that has been cut out but never glued.

This matters because a volume mesher fills a region, and a bag of surfaces does not define one. Hand this file to a mesher that only understands solids and you get either a hard error or, worse, a surface mesh with zero tetrahedra in it and no explanation.

KoFEM repairs this at import, before anything else touches the shape. The loose faces are sewn together with a tolerance scaled to the model size — here 0.0109 mm, one part in ten thousand of the 109 mm bounding-box diagonal — and any shell that comes out watertight is promoted to a solid. The log says so explicitly: 

[occt] sew_faces_into_solid: built 1 solid(s) from 31 surface faces
       (sew tol=0.01092 mm)
[occt] tessellate: bbox diag=109.237 mm -> linear_defl=0.1092 mm

One solid, from 31 loose faces. That line is worth reading every time you import an IGES file: if it reports that it sewed the faces but found no watertight shell, the geometry has a genuine gap and no amount of meshing effort will fix it — you need a better export.

Why the tolerance is relative

A fixed absolute sewing tolerance is wrong at both ends: too tight and the sub-millimetre gaps at IGES trim boundaries never close; too loose and it merges features that are genuinely separate — a thin wall, a small clearance. Scaling with the bounding box keeps the same behaviour on a 20 mm part and a 900 mm casting.

Meshing

With a solid in hand, Netgen meshes it directly from the CAD topology — feature edges first, then the boundary surfaces, then the tetrahedral fill. The classical route for this part, and the one the original course notes take, is to segment the solid by hand and sweep a hexahedral mesh through each block. That produces a beautiful mesh and takes an afternoon. An automatic tetrahedral mesh takes about eight seconds and, as we are about to see, is perfectly adequate provided you pick the right element.

A 5 mm maximum element size gives 8,111 nodes and 33,602 tetrahedra, with automatic refinement around the bore and the fillets where curvature demands it.

Boundary conditions

This is where the analysis is actually decided, so it is worth being explicit about both choices.

Clamp the mounting face

All three translations are fixed on the plate's seating face — the underside at y = −20 mm, 630 mm² of it. That is the face pressed against the support by the two bolts.

Load the bore

Fw = 1000 N is applied to the cylindrical bore of the eye as a surface force, not a point load: KoFEM spreads the total force over the bore's area as a uniform traction and integrates it into work-equivalent nodal forces. The magnitude is arbitrary — the problem is linear, so kw comes out the same for 1 N or 10 kN.

Read the displacement back

w is the mean displacement of the bore surface along the load direction — averaging over the loaded face rather than picking one node, so the number does not depend on which node happened to land where.

What the clamp is really saying

A fully fixed face is an infinitely rigid support. A real bolted joint is not: the plate can lift slightly between the bolts, and the support itself deflects. So this model will be stiffer than the real bracket, on top of any discretisation error. If you needed the true assembly stiffness, you would model the bolts and the support — but for comparing design variants of the bracket itself, a consistent clamp is exactly the right choice.

The first answer, and why it is wrong

Solve it with the default linear tetrahedra at 5 mm elements and you get w = 0.0851 mm, so kw ≈ 11,750 N/mm. It looks like a perfectly respectable result. It is roughly 15 % too stiff.

Refining does not rescue it. Taking the linear mesh from 5,700 nodes all the way to 12,700 — more than doubling it — moves the stiffness from 11,890 down to 11,364 N/mm, a 4.4 % drift that is still going when you run out of patience. The mesh is not the problem; the element is.

The bracket works in bending. A 4-node tetrahedron has constant strain throughout its volume and physically cannot bend, so it resists deformation the mesh should have allowed. Switching the element order to 2 — quadratic shape functions, a mid-side node on every edge — lets strain vary within each element, and the artificial stiffness disappears:

All stiffnesses in N/mm. The quadratic column varies by 0.8 % across the whole range and does not trend — that scatter is re-meshing noise, not convergence error, because each row is an independently generated mesh rather than a refinement of the one above it. That is what a converged answer looks like.

Result
kw ≈ 10,100 N/mm

Quadratic elements, converged across five meshes from 5,700 to 9,500 nodes (10,103–10,185 N/mm). At Fw = 1000 N the eye moves w ≈ 0.099 mm. The linear-tetrahedron answer of ~11,750 N/mm overstates the stiffness by about 15 %.

A note on the stress

Peak von Mises stress across all those runs bounced between 165 and 187 MPa with no pattern — it went up on some refinements and down on others. That is not solver noise, and it is not something to fix by meshing harder.

The maximum sits at the edge of the clamped face, where a perfectly rigid support meets a deformable part. In the exact elasticity solution the stress there is infinite; every mesh simply reports a finite number that depends on how small its elements happen to be at that edge. Refining chases a value that does not exist.

The stiffness, by contrast, is an integral quantity and is completely unbothered by a local singularity — which is exactly why kw converged cleanly while the peak stress did not. If you needed a real stress number here, you would model the fillet radius at the plate, or replace the rigid clamp with a bolted-joint representation, and evaluate away from the corner. See what converges and what does not for the general version of this trap.

What to take away

  1. An IGES file with no solid in it is normal, not broken. Sewing the surfaces into a watertight solid is a standard repair — but check the log line that says it worked.
  2. Element order beat mesh refinement, by a lot. Doubling the linear mesh bought 4.4 %; switching to quadratic elements on the coarsest mesh bought 15 % and landed on the converged answer immediately.
  3. Converged does not mean correct. The rigid clamp still makes this stiffer than the real bolted bracket. Mesh convergence bounds discretisation error and says nothing at all about modelling error.
  4. Report what converged. kw is defensible; the 187 MPa peak is a mesh size wearing a stress's clothes.
