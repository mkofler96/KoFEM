What a finite element solver is actually doing

Every FE package hides the same handful of ideas behind a progress bar. This article walks through them in the order the solver executes them — and ends with the two facts that explain most of the wrong answers people get out of FEA.

The problem you cannot solve

Linear elastostatics is three equations in three unknown displacement fields. Written compactly, you are looking for a displacement field u(x, y, z) that satisfies

div σ(u) + b = 0  in Ω σ is the stress tensor, b the body force, Ω the volume of the part

together with Hooke's law relating σ to the strains, prescribed displacements on part of the boundary, and prescribed tractions on the rest. For a cylinder, a flat plate or a beam, this has a closed-form solution and has had one for a century. For a cast bracket with fillets, a bolt pattern and a bore, it does not — and it never will.

The finite element method does not solve that problem. It solves a different, finite-dimensional problem whose answer converges to it as you refine.

Step one: chop the part into elements

The volume is filled with simple, non-overlapping shapes — tetrahedra, in KoFEM's case, produced by Netgen from the CAD geometry. The corners of those elements are nodes, and the displacement of every node is an unknown. Three unknowns per node, in 3D. This is the entire discretisation: instead of a displacement at infinitely many points, you carry one at a few thousand.

A mesh with 10,000 nodes is a problem with 30,000 unknowns. That is large but entirely finite, and finite is the whole point.

Step two: interpolate between the nodes

The nodes only carry displacement at discrete points, but stress depends on the gradient of displacement, which needs a field. So each element is given an assumed shape: inside element e, the displacement is interpolated from that element's own node values by shape functions Ni.

u(x) = Σi Ni(x) · ui ui is the displacement of node i; Ni is 1 at node i and 0 at every other node

For a 4-node tetrahedron the shape functions are linear, so the displacement varies linearly across the element and its gradient — the strain — is constant within each element. That is a blunt instrument, and the consequences show up later in this article. A 10-node tetrahedron uses quadratic shape functions, which lets strain vary linearly inside the element.

This is the approximation

Everything else in the method is exact bookkeeping. The single place where reality is traded away is here: you have declared that the displacement field looks piecewise-polynomial. If the true solution cannot be represented that way — and it never exactly can — your answer is wrong by the difference.

Step three: build the stiffness matrix

With a displacement field assumed, strain follows by differentiation (ε = B ue, where B holds the shape-function derivatives) and stress follows from Hooke's law (σ = D ε, where D is built from E and ν). Requiring that internal and external virtual work balance for any admissible virtual displacement gives each element a stiffness matrix:

ke = ∫Ωe BT D B dV evaluated numerically at a few Gauss points per element

Read it physically: ke says what forces appear at the element's nodes when you push its nodes around. It depends on the element's shape, its size, and the material — and on nothing else.

Each element's matrix is then added into a global matrix K at the rows and columns belonging to its nodes. Nodes shared between elements accumulate contributions from all of them, which is exactly what enforces continuity of the structure. The result is one big, symmetric, extremely sparse system:

K u = f K: stiffness (n×n) · u: unknown nodal displacements · f: nodal forces

A truss problem solved by hand has the same equation with a 4×4 matrix. A bracket has the same equation with a 30,000×30,000 matrix. There is no conceptual difference, only bookkeeping — and the bookkeeping is what the computer is for.

Step four: apply the boundary conditions

As assembled, K is singular. Nothing in it says where the part is held, so the structure is free to translate and rotate as a rigid body — six ways to move with zero force, six zero eigenvalues, no unique solution. Constraints are not a detail you add for realism; without them there is no answer at all.

  • Essential (Dirichlet) conditions prescribe displacement. Fixing a face sets those nodal DOFs to zero and removes them from the system. Whatever force is needed to hold them is produced as a reaction.
  • Natural (Neumann) conditions prescribe traction. A pressure or a face force is integrated over the loaded surface into the equivalent nodal forces that make up f. A surface with nothing applied to it is automatically traction-free — you do not declare that.
Where the model is really decided

Mesh density gets the attention, but boundary conditions decide the answer. A face you clamp is infinitely rigid, and no real bolted joint is. A force you apply to one node is a point load on a continuum, where stress is genuinely infinite. Most FEA that is badly wrong is not badly meshed — it is badly restrained.

Step five: solve, then differentiate back

With constraints applied, K is symmetric positive definite and the system is solved for u — in KoFEM by a preconditioned conjugate-gradient iteration in MFEM, which never forms the inverse and exploits the sparsity.

Displacements come out first, and they are the primary result. Strains are then recovered by differentiating the displacement field, and stresses from strains. That order matters for how much you trust each one: differentiation loses accuracy, so stress is always a rougher quantity than displacement on the same mesh. With linear tetrahedra, stress is constant within each element, and the jumps between neighbouring elements are a direct measure of discretisation error — which is why a contour plot is smoothed for display and why the smoothing can flatter a bad mesh.

Why the answer comes out too stiff

This is the single most useful fact about displacement-based FEM, and it is rarely stated plainly.

By restricting displacement to piecewise polynomials, you have constrained the structure to deform only in the shapes your mesh can represent. The true structure has every shape available to it and will always find one that stores less energy for the same load. A constrained structure is a stiffer structure. The computed displacement is too small and the computed stiffness is too high. Refining the mesh removes constraint, so displacements grow and stiffness falls toward the exact answer. Your result approaches the truth from one side. (The bound is strict when each mesh is a refinement of the last. Re-meshing a CAD model from scratch at a new element size — what most solvers actually do, KoFEM included — adds some scatter on top of the trend.)

This is genuinely useful. If a mesh refinement makes your part softer by 5 %, you have not found a bug — you have measured your discretisation error, and you know the exact answer is softer still. If refinement makes a result jump around unpredictably instead, something else is going on: a singularity, a contact condition, or a mesh that got worse rather than finer.

Linear tetrahedra are the worst offender

The constant-strain 4-node tetrahedron is the cheapest 3D element and by far the stiffest per node. It cannot represent bending within an element at all: pure bending needs strain to vary across the thickness, and a constant-strain element must fake it with a stack of elements. In a part that works mainly in bending — which is most brackets — a linear-tet mesh can be badly over-stiff even when it looks nicely refined.

The fix is not usually more elements, it is better ones. Second- order (quadratic) elements carry mid-side nodes and let strain vary linearly, and they capture bending on a far coarser mesh. In the hinge bracket example, switching from linear to quadratic elements on the very same mesh dropped the computed stiffness by about 15 % — a bigger correction than refining the linear mesh from 5,700 to 12,700 nodes managed, and it was still not converged after that.

What converges, and what does not

"Refine until it stops changing" is good advice applied to the wrong quantity about half the time.

  • Displacements and stiffness converge well. They are integrals of the solution, they approach from one side, and a two- or three-level mesh study pins them down.
  • Stress away from features converges. In a smooth region with several elements through the thickness, stress is trustworthy.
  • Peak stress at a re-entrant corner does not converge. At a sharp internal corner, the edge of a clamped face, or a point load, the exact elasticity solution is genuinely singular — stress goes to infinity. Refining the mesh does not approach a number, it climbs. If your acceptance criterion is the maximum von Mises stress in the model and your model has a sharp internal corner, you are reporting a mesh size, not a stress.

The practical answer to a singularity is to model the fillet that really exists there, or to evaluate stress at a defined distance from the corner, or to use a criterion that does not depend on the peak. What does not work is refining until the number looks reasonable.

A short checklist

  1. Know which quantity you are after. Stiffness and peak stress demand very different meshes and very different scepticism.
  2. Check the restraints first. Ask what the clamped face is standing in for, and whether infinite rigidity flatters your answer.
  3. Run at least two mesh densities. One number is not a result — a trend is.
  4. Prefer second-order elements for anything that bends, before reaching for a finer mesh.
  5. Sanity-check against hand calculation. A beam formula that lands within a factor of two is worth more than three significant figures from a model nobody checked.
