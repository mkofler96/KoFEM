Consistent unit systems

Which combinations of units can be used together in a finite element model?

What consistency means

A consistent system makes the governing equations work directly on the numerical values, without hidden conversion factors. Choose mass, length and time units; force, stress, energy and density then follow. With square brackets denoting the unit of a quantity:

[F]=[M][L][T]2.

Here F is force, M is mass, L is length and T is time. Stress and Young’s modulus have force-per-area units; energy has force-times-length units; mass density has mass-per-volume units:

[σ]=[E]=[F][L]2,[W]=[F][L],[ρ]=[M][L]3.

The symbols σ, E, W and ρ denote stress, Young’s modulus, energy and mass density respectively. One can instead choose force, length and time and derive the mass unit. This is why N–mm–s uses tonnes, while N–mm–ms uses grams.

For the general method, see Barenblatt’s textbook and Buckingham’s dimensional-analysis paper. Matching dimensions is a necessary check on an equation; it cannot by itself establish the physical model or determine a dimensionless factor.

A tonne here is a metric tonne, not a US short ton. In the inch system, lbf·s²/in is a mass unit; it is not a pound mass (lbm). Replacing it by lbm would require an additional conversion factor in force–mass relations.

Even in a static calculation that has no mass or time term, geometry, loads and stiffness must agree. For N and mm, 1 MPa=1 N/mm2; for kN and mm, 1 GPa=1 kN/mm2.

The default stress unit changes with the unit system. In SI with metres, kilograms and seconds, stress and Young’s modulus are expressed in Pa (N/m²) by default. In an N–mm system, the corresponding unit is MPa (N/mm²). Switching to SI therefore changes the numerical values of both material stiffness and reported stress: a modulus of 210000 MPa becomes 2.10×1011 Pa, and a stress of 100 MPa becomes 108 Pa. This is a unit conversion, not a change in the physical material or stress.

The same material in different units

The two N–mm systems share the same force, stress and energy units. Their mass-density numbers differ by a factor of one million because their mass units differ by that factor. A density value cannot be copied between these systems unchanged.

Practical checks

  • Record the unit system before importing geometry or entering material values.
  • Convert every dimensional quantity, including thickness, pressure, moments and prescribed displacements. Converting length alone changes the problem.
  • Keep dimensionless quantities, such as Poisson’s ratio and strain, unchanged.
  • Check a simple calculation in two systems and convert the outputs back to the same unit. They should represent the same physical answer.

For example, the bar in Model and units has length 1 m, area 0.0001 m², force 1000 N and modulus 210×109 Pa in SI. Its extension is approximately 4.7619×10−5 m=0.047619 mm in either system.

Example: blast-wave scaling

The famous historical example is G. I. Taylor’s analysis of the 1945 Trinity atomic test, published in two 1950 papers. The dimensional argument below captures the scaling of an expanding shock front. Taylor’s full analysis also used flow equations and observations; units alone were not the entire calculation.

Consider an idealised, strong, spherical blast in a uniform medium. Suppose the shock-front radius R depends on released energy Q, ambient mass density ρ and elapsed time t. Here Q denotes energy, keeping it distinct from Young’s modulus E above. Their dimensions are:

[Q]=[M][L]2[T]−2,[ρ]=[M][L]−3,[t]=[T].

Write a candidate power law R=CQaρbtc, with a dimensionless coefficient C. Matching mass, length and time powers on both sides gives:

a+b=0,2a−3b=1,−2a+c=0.

Thus a=1/5, b=−1/5 and c=2/5, giving the familiar Sedov–Taylor scaling:

R=C(Qt2ρ)1/5.

Dimensional analysis fixes these exponents under the stated assumptions. It does not determine C, which requires a physical solution or measurement. The model assumes a concentrated energy release and negligible ambient pressure and energy losses. Its radius is the position of a shock front at a given time, not a damage radius.
