Why does KoFEM solve the weak form instead of pointwise equilibrium?

Linear elasticity links displacement, strain and stress. Two ingredients remain: force balance, and the conditions on the body’s surface. Together they form a boundary-value problem. This page states it, then rewrites it as the weak form that the finite element method discretises. The same static, small-strain, isotropic assumptions apply. Units are N, mm and MPa.

Equilibrium inside the body

Take any piece V of the body, with boundary ∂V and outward normal n. In a static state, the tractions on its surface and the body force b (N/mm³) inside it must add to zero:

∫∂VσndS+∫VbdV=0.

The divergence theorem turns the surface integral into ∫V∇·σdV. Because the balance holds for every piece, however small, the integrand itself must vanish wherever the stress is smooth:

∇·σ+b=0 in Ω,∂σij∂xj+bi=0.

Each of the three equations has units MPa/mm = N/mm³. The stress divergence takes the divergence of each row of σ. Balance of moments adds the symmetry σij=​σji already used on the previous page.

Boundary conditions and signs

Equilibrium alone has infinitely many solutions; the surface fixes which one applies. At every boundary point and in each direction, exactly one of two quantities is prescribed. The boundary Γ splits accordingly:

u=uD on ΓD,σn=t on ΓN.
  • Displacement (Dirichlet, essential) on ΓD: a fixed support is uD=​0; a prescribed displacement is a nonzero uD in mm.
  • Traction (Neumann, natural) on ΓN: t in MPa. A free surface is t=​0; it is the default wherever nothing is applied.
  • Mixed by component: a frictionless roller on a face with normal z prescribes uz=​0 and zero traction in x and y.

The sign follows the outward normal. A positive tx on a face whose outward normal is +x pulls the body outward. A pressure pload is positive when it pushes into the surface, so t=​−ploadn. A total face force F becomes the uniform traction F/A over the face area A; Loads and constraints works through that conversion.

The complete boundary-value problem

Collecting the pieces gives the strong form: find a displacement u such that

∇·σ(u)+b=0 in Ω,u=uD on ΓD,σ(u)n=t on ΓN,

where σ(u)=​λ(∇·u)I+2με(u) is the isotropic law applied to the strain of u. Substituting it shows the cost: equilibrium contains second derivatives of displacement.

The uniform bar of the previous page satisfies every line. Its stress is constant, so ∇·σ=​0. On the loaded end n=​(1,0,0) and σn=​(100,0,0) MPa, the applied P/A. The sides have σyy=​σzz=​0, so they are traction-free. Only a fully clamped end breaks this: it forbids the lateral contraction the uniform solution needs, so near that end the true 3D field is not uniform.

Why not solve equilibrium pointwise?

The finite element method approximates u with shape functions that are continuous across elements but whose slopes jump at shared faces. Such a field has no second derivative there, so the strong form cannot even be evaluated. Real models have the same difficulty:

  • where two bodies with different materials meet, strain, and hence some stress components, jump; only the traction σn across the interface is continuous;
  • at re-entrant corners and under concentrated loads, stress is unbounded; and
  • traction boundary conditions would have to be imposed as separate equations on derivatives at surface points.

The weak form avoids all three. It needs only first derivatives, it handles material interfaces without extra conditions, and traction conditions enter automatically as work terms. When the solution is smooth, it is equivalent to the strong form.

Derive the weak form

Choose a test displacement w (mm): any sufficiently regular field that is zero on ΓD. Think of it as a virtual displacement compatible with the supports. Take the dot product of equilibrium with w and integrate over the body:

∫Ωw·(∇·σ)dV+∫Ωw·bdV=0.

Integration by parts moves the derivative from σ to w and produces a boundary term:

∫Ωw·(∇·σ)dV=∫Γw·(σn)dS−∫Ω∇w:σdV.

Now apply the boundary conditions. On ΓD the test displacement is zero, so that part of the boundary integral drops out. On ΓN replace σn by the prescribed t. Because σ is symmetric, ∇w:σ=​ε(w):σ. The result is the principle of virtual work:

∫Ωε(w):σ(u)dV=∫Ωw·bdV+∫ΓNw·tdS.

Every term is work, in N·mm. The traction condition no longer appears as a separate equation; it is satisfied naturally by any solution, hence the name. The displacement condition is not in the equation at all; it is essential and must be built into the space of trial displacements. A zero test field on ΓD does not make uD zero.

Bilinear and linear forms

Writing the two sides as separate functions gives the standard abstract statement. Define the trial set and the test space

U={u:u=uD on ΓD},W0={w:w=0 on ΓD},

both restricted to fields with finite strain energy (first derivatives that are square-integrable). Then find u∈U with

a(u,w)=ℓ(w)for all w∈W0,
a(u,w)=∫Ω[λ(∇·u)(∇·w)+2με(u):ε(w)]dV,
ℓ(w)=∫Ωw·bdV+∫ΓNw·tdS.

The bilinear form a is the isotropic law inserted into internal virtual work, using trε(u)=​∇·u. It is linear in each argument and symmetric: a(u,w)=​a(w,u). The linear form ℓ collects the loads. Replacing u and w by shape-function expansions turns a into the stiffness matrix K and ℓ into the load vector f. A concentrated force P applied at a node adds P·w at that node. It is admissible only after discretisation: in the continuous problem a point force produces infinite displacement beneath it.

Uniqueness and minimum potential energy

Setting w=​u shows a(u,u)=​2∫ΩWdV, twice the strain energy. It is zero only for rigid-body motions: three translations and three rotations. If the displacement conditions exclude those motions, a(w,w)>​0 for every nonzero admissible w. Korn’s inequality turns this into a lower bound, and the weak form then has exactly one solution. Without enough supports, the discrete matrix is singular; Rigid-body modes shows what happens.

Because a is symmetric and positive, the weak form is also the condition for the minimum of the total potential energy

Π(u)=12a(u,u)−ℓ(u).

Of all displacements that satisfy the supports, the elastic solution has the least stored energy minus load work. This explains why the discrete stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite and why the conjugate gradient method applies.

Worked check: a two-material bar

A one-dimensional bar shows the weak form at work, including a material interface. It is 1000 mm long with section A=​104 mm²: steel (E1=​210000 MPa) for 0≤​x≤​500 mm, aluminium (E2=​70000 MPa) for 500≤​x≤​1000 mm. The left end is fixed, u(0)=​0; the right end carries P=​106 N. There is no body force. In one dimension the weak form reads:

∫0LEAu′w′dx=Pw(L)for all w with w(0)=0.

Try a displacement with constant force N=​EAu′ in each segment, so u′=​N/(E1A) or N/(E2A). Then the left side is ∫0LNw′dx=​N[w(L)−w(0)]=​Nw(L). The equation holds for every admissible w exactly when N=​P. The weak form imposed the end load without any separate boundary equation. It also needed no interface condition. The displacement is continuous but has a kink at 500 mm, where the strong form has no classical second derivative.

Two-material bar, P=​106 N, A=​104 mm²
SegmentStress σ=​P/AStrain σ/EExtension over 500 mm
Steel100 MPa1/2100≈​4.762×10−40.2381 mm
Aluminium100 MPa1/700≈​1.429×10−30.7143 mm

The stress is continuous; the strain jumps by a factor of three. The tip displacement is 500/2100+500/700=​20/21≈​0.9524 mm. Units check: EA is N, u′w′ is 1, dx is mm, so both sides are N·mm. Order-1 elements whose nodes include x=​500 mm reproduce this solution exactly, because it is piecewise linear. In 3D, a free lateral surface would also contract differently in each material near the interface, which this 1D model ignores.

KoFEM implementation

KoFEM uses MFEM 4.7. The solid solver builds a and ℓ directly.

  • Bilinear form. MFEM’s elasticity integrator implements exactly a(u,w) above (MFEM ElasticityIntegrator). KoFEM passes it piecewise-constant λ and μ, one pair per body (solve_mfem.cpp). The mixed solid–shell solve uses the same integrator for its solid part (solve_coupled.cpp).
  • Linear form. Face forces become uniform tractions F/A, tractions are applied directly, and pressures as −ploadn (fem_bc_io.cpp). They are integrated against the test functions on the selected faces (fem_bc_io.cpp). Nodal forces are added to the load vector (fem_bc_io.cpp). There is no body-force term: b=​0, so self-weight is not modelled.
  • Essential conditions. Fixed and prescribed translation components are collected (solve_mfem.cpp), their values are written into the solution, and the constrained equations are eliminated (solve_mfem.cpp). This is how u∈U and w∈W0 are enforced.

The shell path solves a different weak form with rotations; see Shell formulation. The axial bar example runs this complete solid path in the browser engine.

Decision: a displacement formulation

  • Choice: KoFEM solves the weak form for displacement only (a primal, or irreducible, Galerkin formulation), with continuous shape functions for u and the isotropic law above.
  • Alternatives: collocation or finite differences on the strong form; mixed displacement–pressure formulations; stress-based or hybrid elements; B-bar and reduced-integration variants; nonlinear formulations for large deformation or plasticity.
  • Why it fits: it needs only first derivatives and two material constants, imposes tractions and material interfaces naturally, and yields a symmetric positive definite system suited to a conjugate gradient solve in the browser. MFEM provides it as a standard, well-tested integrator.
  • Tradeoffs: stress comes from derivatives of the solution and is less accurate than displacement; it is discontinuous between elements. Linear tetrahedra can lock in bending and with ν near ½.
  • Revisit when: nearly incompressible materials, large deformation, plasticity or contact become supported features, or when stress accuracy in thin sections needs a different element.

KoFEM’s architecture decision records cover the C++/WASM engine as the production pipeline (ADR-0001), not this formulation. It is the standard formulation for linear-elastic solid FEM and has no separate record.

Limitations and practical consequences

  • The weak form is only as good as the model: it inherits every linear-elastic limit.
  • Point forces and point supports are acceptable only as discrete loads. Refining the mesh near them makes local stress grow without bound; spread a load over a realistic area.
  • A fixed support on a whole face also blocks lateral contraction. It adds stiffness and local stress that a real joint may not have.
  • Supports that leave a rigid-body motion free make the problem unsolvable. Supports that block more than needed change the answer.
  • Gravity and other body forces are not available in the current solver.
