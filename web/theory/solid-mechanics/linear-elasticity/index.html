Which assumptions lead from displacement to isotropic linear-elastic stress?

A linear-elastic model describes a solid that deforms a little under load and returns to its original shape when the load is removed. Three fields describe it: how far each point moves, how much the material is stretched or sheared, and the internal force per area. This page links them. Equilibrium and weak form then adds force balance and boundary conditions. Units are N, mm and MPa (N/mm²), as in the consistent-unit reference.

Assumptions and scope

The model on this page, and KoFEM’s solid solver, assume:

  • Static: loads are applied slowly; inertia and damping are ignored.
  • Small displacement and small strain: the deformed shape is close enough to the original that all equations are written on the undeformed body, and strains are much smaller than 1.
  • Linear elastic: stress is proportional to strain, with no yielding, damage, creep or rate dependence. Removing the load removes all deformation.
  • Isotropic and piecewise homogeneous: the stiffness is the same in every direction and constant within each body.
  • Stress-free reference: no initial stress, preload or thermal strain.
  • Fully three-dimensional: no plane-stress, plane-strain or axisymmetric reduction.

Positions use a fixed Cartesian basis x1=​x, x2=​y, x3=​z; repeated indices are summed, as in the notation reference. Shells use a reduced form of the same material law, described in Shell formulation.

From displacement to small strain

The displacement u(x) (mm) moves the point originally at position x to x+u. Displacement alone says nothing about deformation: a body can translate without stretching. Deformation is measured by how displacement changes from point to point, its gradient ∇u, with components ∂ui/∂xj.

The small-strain tensor is the symmetric part of that gradient:

ε=12(∇u+(∇u)T),εij=12(∂ui∂xj+∂uj∂xi).

Strain is dimensionless (mm/mm). The diagonal entries εxx,εyy,εzz are stretches along the axes, positive when a fibre lengthens. Their sum, the trace trε=​∇·u, is the relative change in volume. The off-diagonal entries are shear: 2εxy is the decrease in the right angle between fibres along x and y. That factor of 2 is the engineering shear strain γxy.

The antisymmetric part of ∇u is a small rigid rotation. Dropping it means rigid translations and small rigid rotations produce no strain. Gradient and divergence works through this split for a concrete field.

Why small rotations matter

The symmetric gradient is a linearisation. It is exact for rigid translations but only approximate for a rigid rotation. Rotate a body by angle θ about z, without deforming it. Then ux=​(cosθ−1)x−(sinθ)y and uy=​(sinθ)x+(cosθ−1)y, so

εxx=εyy=cosθ−1≈−θ22,εxy=0.

A rigid rotation should give zero strain. The error grows with θ2: at θ=​0.01 rad (0.57°) it is −5×10−5; at θ=​0.1 rad (5.7°) it is about −5.0×10−3, ten times the working strain of the steel bar checked below. A slender part that visibly rotates under load, such as a long cantilever or a thin flexible strip, therefore violates the small-strain assumption even when the material itself stretches very little.

Stress and traction

Cut the body by an imaginary surface with unit normal n. The force per area that one side exerts on the other is the traction t (N/mm² = MPa). Cauchy’s stress theorem says a single tensor, the stress σ, gives the traction for every cut direction:

t=σn,ti=σijnj.

Component σij is the force in direction i per unit area of a face whose outward normal points along j. Tension is positive: σxx>​0 pulls a face with n=​(1,0,0) outward, and the opposite face, n=​(−1,0,0), is pulled the other way. Balance of moments on a small cube makes the stress symmetric, σij=​σji, so it has six independent components. The scalar von Mises stress summarises them for yield checks; it is not a tensor component and has no sign.

The isotropic linear-elastic law

A linear-elastic material maps strain to stress linearly. For an isotropic material, two constants suffice. With the Lamé parameters λ and μ (MPa) and the identity tensor I:

σ=λ(trε)I+2με,σij=λεkkδij+2μεij.

The first term responds only to volume change and acts equally in all directions; the second responds to every strain component. δij is the Kronecker delta. Engineers usually specify Young’s modulus E and Poisson’s ratio ν instead, which are measured directly in a tension test. Inverting the law in those terms gives:

ε=1+νEσ−νE(trσ)I.

In uniaxial stress σxx this reads εxx=​σxx/E and εyy=​εzz=​−νσxx/E: E is the axial stiffness, ν the ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension. In Voigt form, with the engineering-shear order xx,yy,zz,yz,xz,xy used in Elements and quadrature:

D=[λ+2μλλ000λλ+2μλ000λλλ+2μ000000μ000000μ000000μ]

The shear entries are μ, not 2μ, because the Voigt vector stores engineering shear γij=​2εij: σxy=​μγxy. D has units MPa and is symmetric.

Any two of the common constants determine the rest:

A stable material stores positive energy for every nonzero strain. That requires μ>​0 and K>​0, which for E and ν means

E>0,−1<ν<12.

As ν approaches ½ the material becomes incompressible: K and λ grow without bound while μ stays finite. At ν=​0.49, λ/μ=​49; at ν=​0.3 it is 1.5. Rubber and other nearly incompressible materials therefore need special element formulations; the standard displacement elements discussed on the next pages become too stiff (volumetric locking). Most metals have ν near 0.3; ν=​0 and negative values are physically possible.

Worked check: a bar in uniaxial tension

A steel bar 1000 mm long, with a 100 mm × 100 mm section (A=​104 mm²), carries an axial force P=​106 N spread uniformly over each end. Its sides are free. Away from local end effects, the stress is uniaxial and uniform:

σxx=PA=106 N104 mm2=100 MPa.

The inverse law gives the strains. Units cancel to a pure number because MPa/MPa = 1:

εxx=100210000=12100≈4.762×10−4,εyy=εzz=−0.3εxx=−17000.

Now check the forward law with the Lamé constants from the table, written exactly as λ=​1575000/13 MPa and μ=​1050000/13 MPa. The trace is trε=​(1−2ν)εxx=​1/5250. Then

σxx=λtrε+2μεxx=30013+100013=100 MPa,
σyy=λtrε+2μεyy=30013−30013=0.

The law reproduces the applied stress and leaves the free sides stress-free, as uniaxial tension requires. The resulting displacements are:

  • extension δ=​εxxL=​1000/2100≈​0.4762 mm, which is the familiar δ=​PL/(EA);
  • lateral contraction of each 100 mm side: 100×(−1/7000)≈​−0.0143 mm; and
  • von Mises stress: 100 MPa, equal to σxx in uniaxial stress.

A shear check is equally short: an engineering shear strain γxy=​10−3 gives σxy=​μγxy≈​80.8 MPa, with no normal stress. The axial-bar verification compares the same bar with KoFEM’s full solve. Its fixed end also stops the lateral contraction locally, which the uniform solution above ignores.

Strain energy

Elastic deformation stores energy. Per unit volume, the strain energy density is

W=12σ:ε=λ2(trε)2+με:ε,

using the double contraction σ:ε=​σijεij. Its units are MPa = N·mm/mm³, energy per volume. For the bar, W=​12×100×12100=​142 N·mm/mm³. Over the volume 107 mm³, the stored energy is about 238095 N·mm, equal to 12Pδ, the work done by the slowly applied load. The admissible ranges above make W positive for every nonzero strain. That positivity is what later makes the stiffness matrix of a supported model positive definite.

Where the model stops applying

The model is appropriate for stiff parts in service: small, reversible deformation of metals, rigid plastics and similar materials below their elastic limit. It answers “how far does it move and how high is the stress?”, not “when does it fail?”. Its assumptions stop holding when:

  • the stress exceeds the elastic limit. A linear model keeps scaling stress with load; it never yields. A computed von Mises stress above the yield strength means the real part would deform plastically and the computed distribution is no longer valid there.
  • rotations or displacements are large. Flexible strips, long cantilevers and snap-fits need a geometrically nonlinear analysis. Linear analysis also cannot predict buckling.
  • the material is not isotropic. Fibre composites, wood, rolled sheet with strong texture and many 3D-printed parts have direction-dependent stiffness.
  • the material is nearly incompressible, such as rubber or many elastomers, with ν close to ½.
  • other strains or stresses exist: temperature change, bolt preload, residual stress, creep, or time- and rate-dependent behaviour.
  • loads are dynamic, such as impact or vibration near resonance.

KoFEM implementation

  • Each material stores E (MPa), ν and a density. The material form rejects E≤​0 and ν outside (−1,0.5) (MaterialsPanel.tsx). Density is stored but does not enter the static solve.
  • The solid solver requires E and ν for every material and selects one material per element from the element’s body (solve_mfem.cpp). The engine itself does not check their range; that check exists only in the app form.
  • It converts each material to λ and μ with the formulas above (solve_mfem.cpp); the mixed solid–shell solve does the same for its solid elements (solve_coupled.cpp).
  • Stress for the von Mises result uses the same law, σij=​λεkkδij+2μεij (solve_mfem.cpp). Displacement and stress explains where it is sampled.

KoFEM has no thermal, plastic, anisotropic, hyperelastic or geometrically nonlinear material model, and no separate treatment for nearly incompressible materials.
