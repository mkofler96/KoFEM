Gradient and divergence

How do derivatives connect displacement, strain, stress and equilibrium?

A field varies from point to point. A gradient records that variation; a divergence measures its net outward flow. We use a fixed Cartesian basis with x1=​x, x2=​y and x3=​z, following the index convention. The polynomial examples below use dimensionless coordinates and values unless units are shown. They illustrate operators, not solved models.

Gradient of a scalar

The gradient turns a scalar field q into a 3-vector of slopes. A component gives the change per unit distance along that coordinate axis:

(∇q)i=∂q∂xi,∇q=(∂q/∂x,∂q/∂y,∂q/∂z)T.

For q=​x2+3y, the derivatives are (2x,3,0). At (2,1,0), q=​7 and ∇q=​(4,3,0)T. A small step h along x changes q by approximately 4h; a step along z does not change it. The gradient points toward the steepest local increase.

Units divide by length: a pressure gradient has units MPa/mm. In FEM, the gradient of a dimensionless shape function Na has units 1/mm. These derivatives turn nodal displacements into strain.

Gradient of a vector

A 3-vector field v has three slopes for each of its three components. Its gradient is a 3 × 3 tensor. Row i identifies the vector component; column j identifies the differentiation direction.

(∇v)ij=∂vi∂xj.

Take the displacement field

u=(0.001x+0.002y,−0.004x+0.003y,0.005z)T,

with coordinates and displacement both measured in mm. Its dimensionless gradient is constant:

∇u=[0.0010.0020−0.0040.0030000.005]

The xy entry is ∂ux/∂y=​0.002; the yx entry is ∂uy/∂x=​−0.004. Some texts transpose this convention. Check the component definition before transferring a formula. In an element, differentiating uih=​∑aNadai gives (∇uh)ij=​∑adai∂Na/∂xj: each nodal coefficient multiplies a shape-function slope.

Small strain and shear

The gradient includes both deformation and local rotation. Its symmetric part gives the infinitesimal strain tensor, a symmetric 3 × 3 output:

εij(u)=12((∇u)ij+(∇u)ji).
ε(u)=[0.001−0.0010−0.0010.0030000.005]

Here εxy=​(0.002−0.004)/2=​−0.001. The engineering shear is γxy=​2εxy=​−0.002. With shear modulus μ=​80000 MPa, σxy=​2μεxy=​μγxy=​−160 MPa. See why shear appears twice in tensor work.

As a check, u=​(−θy,θx,0)T has opposite xy and yx derivatives, so its symmetric gradient is zero. This is an infinitesimal rigid rotation. Small-strain kinematics neglect quadratic gradient terms; finite rotations need a different strain measure.

Divergence of a vector

The divergence maps a 3-vector field to a scalar by adding its outward directional changes:

∇·v=∂vx∂x+∂vy∂y+∂vz∂z=tr(∇v).

For v=​(x2,xy,z)T, ∇·v=​2x+x+1=​3x+1, giving 7 at x=​2. Positive divergence means a small region has more outgoing than incoming flow. For displacement, ∇·u=​trε gives first-order relative volume change: the example above gives 0.009.

Divergence of stress

Stress is a tensor field, so its divergence is a 3-vector. Using the traction convention ti=​σijnj, differentiate and sum across each row:

(∇·σ)i=∑j=13∂σij∂xj.
σ=[x2xy0xy2y0003z]

The x component is 2x+x+0=​3x; the y component is y+2+0; the z component is 3. At (2,1,0), ∇·σ=​(6,3,3)T. This is different from taking the gradient of trσ.

In physical units, differentiating stress (N/mm²) gives N/mm³, matching body force per volume b. Static equilibrium is ∇·σ+b=​0. The weak form transfers this derivative to a test displacement so neighbouring FEM elements need not share a pointwise stress derivative.
