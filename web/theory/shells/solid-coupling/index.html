Solid–shell coupling

How are shell and solid degrees of freedom coupled?

A shell carries translations and rotations at its mid-surface; a solid carries translations throughout a volume. Joining their nodes by position alone does not specify how a bending moment crosses the interface. The connection needs a motion rule whose work-equivalent forces carry the intended load path.

Choose the connection’s physical meaning

A bonded tie idealises a permanent connection. It does not model contact opening, sliding, friction or the compliance of a weld or adhesive layer. In the all-solid solve, KoFEM merges selected node pairs into representative translational nodes. In the coupled assembler, user-declared surface ties instead create distributing constraints across the selected surfaces. The same UI intent therefore uses different discrete approximations on those paths; compare the affected response.

A shell–solid transition replaces part of one continuous wall with a shell. Its section should transmit extension, shear and bending without replacing a three-dimensional joint by an averaged remote point. Keep local solid material where the joint response matters and move the idealised section away from that region.

A surface-to-point coupling models a remote load, attachment or rigid connection. Choose kinematic when a rigid gripped patch is intended, distributing when a patch should retain deformation. Neither choice supplies the detailed stresses of a real bolt, weld or bearing.

Motion constraints also transfer loads

The coupled solve uses six slots per pool node. Solid stiffness occupies the three translational slots; shell stiffness occupies the shell node’s translations and rotations. Unused rotations on solid-only nodes are fixed, except at a coupling reference that needs them. MFEM assembles linear tetrahedral solid stiffness, while the CST/DKT core adds shells.

A constraint expresses dependent coefficients as linear combinations of independent coefficients. Write the full vector as d=​Tq, where q contains independent unknowns and T is the constraint map. Substituting it into virtual work gives:

Kr=TTKT,fr=TTf,Krq=fr.

The transpose is essential: the same motion map determines force and moment transfer. Work consistency alone does not prove a particular map reproduces every rigid motion, local stress field or reference-point eccentricity. Test those claims separately. KoFEM resolves chains to independent coefficients and rejects cycles and conflicting constraint ownership before the solve.

Retain solid material at the joint

At detected planar wall roots, the CAD idealisation retains a solid wall section extending three wall thicknesses from the joint. It clips the solid tetrahedra and shell facets at the transition plane, so the shell begins at a physical through-thickness solid section. This preserves three-dimensional material around the joint and counts the replaced wall volume once. The placement is a current algorithmic choice, not a theorem that three thicknesses suffice for every geometry.

The physical-footprint constraint (engine kind 3) makes each solid section node dependent on the two shell edge nodes. Let N1=​1−s and N2=​s be the linear edge weights at projected coordinate 0≤​s≤​1. Let xa, ua and θa be shell node positions, translations and rotations; xS is the solid node position. Then:

xA=N1x1+N2x2,r=xS−xA,uS=∑a=12Na(ua+θa×r).

This is the shell’s section kinematics applied across its physical thickness. The weights sum to one and reproduce the edge anchor, so a common rigid translation and rotation gives the same motion on both sides. The transpose distributes forces and moments to the shell edge. The solid section cannot warp independently at this cut; place it where the shell assumption is appropriate.

The detector requires a section spanning both wall faces, with solid boundary nodes within half the wall thickness of the shell mid-surface. It interpolates nonmatching node positions along the shell edge. Nearby search-ball seam constraints are excluded where this footprint is active. Other seams can still use the older proximity-based method; this is not an arbitrary surface-to-surface transition implementation.

Distinguish the other coupling methods

Coupling kinematics at the reviewed revision
MethodDependent motionConsequence
Physical footprint (kind 3)Solid section translations follow two interpolated shell-edge nodes and their rotations.Transfers the shell section kinematics over its thickness; suppresses warping at the cut. Used for recognized section footprints.
Relaxed seam MPC (kind 1)All six shell reference coefficients follow a distance-weighted solid-patch centroid translation and a scaled least-squares patch rotation.Localizes the older seam tie. It is not a rigid section footprint and does not tie to an arbitrary nearest face node.
Distributing, RBE3 style (kind 0)Reference translation is a weighted average of patch translations; reference rotation is computed from relative patch motion by the implemented least-squares map.Allows patch deformation; the dependent reference can be loaded, but cannot be directly fixed or driven. Automatic partner sets use equal weights. Patch geometry, reach and eccentricity affect behavior.
Kinematic, RBE2 style (kind 2)Selected target translations follow reference translation plus reference rotation crossed with their offsets.The independent reference can be fixed, driven or loaded. It makes the selected patch rigid. Rotation-mask bits only tie rotations where those DOFs exist.

For the relaxed seam MPC, base weights are multiplied by 1/(di2+ε2), with di the partner distance from the shell reference and ε=​0.05rmax. Translation follows the resulting weighted centroid; rotation follows the weighted fit times relaxation ψ. The app currently sets ψ=​1; it is not a user control. Averaging an equidistant pair on opposite wall faces avoids arbitrarily attaching the mid-surface to one face. This differs from both the physical footprint and generic equal-weight distributing coupling.

A kinematic target translation uses the same rigid-body rule ui=​uR+θR×(xi−xR). Leaving target rotation bits free on a solid surface does not create a hinge: solid nodes have no physical nodal rotations, and their translational patch still follows the rigid reference. A point-to-point constraint with free rotations is a different idealisation.

The historical distributing-junction study found reasonable tip motion alongside severely low local solid stress. The current planar transition addresses that model with retained solid material and a section footprint. It does not establish a general repair to arbitrary distributing patches. See the issue and its evidence.

Materials, loads and constraints

  • Elements and materials: the explicit mixed path accepts CTRIA3 shells and CTETRA solids. Other solid element types are rejected. Solid tetrahedra support distinct per-body materials through per-tet attributes. The shell domain still requires one material ID, with per-facet thickness. The legacy solve-time CAD idealisation requires one material per domain; it does not share that per-tet capability.
  • Loads: face force, traction and pressure become nodal forces. App moment groups normally become force couples. The coupled packer passes direct moment components at declared reference points; it does not pass arbitrary direct moments at ordinary nodes. Confirm the load selection still describes the intended surface after idealisation.
  • Supports: the coupled/idealised app path clamps shell rotations when all three translations are fixed to zero. A prescribed translation does not automatically clamp rotation. Direct driven rotations require a declared reference point on this path. Pure-shell payloads have the separate six-component support described in the shell article.
  • Dependent nodes: the core rejects fixed or prescribed dependent coefficients. The app drops proximity seam ties at constrained shell nodes, but refuses a prescribed/fixed footprint solid node and a tie overlapping that transition. Resolve the competing load paths or retain the body as Solid.
  • Connectivity: separate bodies require shared nodes or a declared connection; proximity alone does not bond an assembly. A declared tie that neither couples nor shares any nodes produces an error. An all-shell model cannot use a declared surface-to-point coupling today; that assembler needs a solid domain.

The result contains shared-pool translations and separate solid and shell element von Mises arrays. Shell values come from the worse surface at each facet centroid; solid values sample the tetrahedron centre. They describe different stress states and sampling locations. Avoid treating a colour discontinuity across the transition as a stress continuity test.

Check the planar wall-on-block case

The existing native study uses a 60 × 40 × 6 mm block with a centred 60 mm long, 30 mm high, 2 mm thick wall. The block bottom is fixed in all translations. All material has E=​210000 MPa and ν=​0.3. A total P=​100 N acts along the wall top, either upward (tension) or along its length (in-plane bending). It compares an all-solid wall with shell transitions at 0, 3 and 6 mm above the block; 6 mm equals three wall thicknesses.

For refinement index r the block has 12r cells along its length, 9r across its nonuniform width and 3r through block depth. The wall has r divisions through thickness and 10r over its height. Cells become linear tetrahedra; the remaining shell is triangulated. The study refines all directions together. The record here executes r=​1,2, with CG relative target 10−10.

The numerical table comes from the native production-core study with a conforming planar footprint. Its separate asymmetric-patch checks concern kinematic coupling rigid motions and six force/moment loads. The shell suite also checks footprint rigid/affine motion and clamped-cantilever moment transfer. The app fixture separately checks clipping, volume, nonmatching edge interpolation and WASM solve behavior. These checks exercise different layers; passing one does not substitute for the others.

What this evidence establishes

The retained planar section reproduces the tested global motion and fixed-region solid stresses closely relative to the same-mesh all-solid model. This is a comparison between two discrete models, not an analytical stress solution or physical validation. The all-solid reference itself changes under refinement. Its sharp T-junction peak is mesh-dependent; peak agreement between the two models does not establish a converged physical peak.

The study covers planar section cuts and in-plane loading. It does not establish arbitrary three-dimensional junction stresses, curved cuts, general distributing coupling, out-of-plane joint response, or accurate extraction of every branched thin-walled section. The T-junction wall-extraction limitation is separate from retaining a solid root in a successfully detected wall.

Choose a quantity and fixed sampling region before comparing models. Check reactions, motion, stress away from the artificial cut and sensitivity to both mesh and transition placement. Keep the region solid when those checks do not support the shell idealisation. See verification strategy and result interpretation.
