Kinematic and distributing couplings

What does a coupling do to the surface it grips, and which one should stand in for a screw, a shaft or a remote load?

A point that stands for a part

A coupling joins one reference point to the nodes of a surface. It lets a part that is not modelled — a screw, a shaft, a loading jig — act on the model through that point: a force or a moment on the point reaches the surface, and a support on the point holds it. The point carries three translations and three rotations, so it can take a moment, which a single solid node cannot.

There are two ways to tie the point to the surface, and they are not interchangeable. A kinematic coupling makes the surface move as a rigid body with the point. A distributing coupling lets the surface deform and makes the point follow its average motion. Both are exact constraints on the discretisation: they enter the system through the same transformation as every other constraint (constraint reduction), and the loads they carry follow from the motion they impose. What differs is that motion.

Below, xR is the reference point and uR, θR its translation and rotation. The coupled nodes i=​1…N sit at xi and move by ui. Rotations are small, as everywhere in the linear analysis.

Kinematic coupling (RBE2)

Every coupled node follows the point as a rigid body:

ui=uR+θR×(xi−xR),i=1…N.

The coupled nodes become dependent; the point stays a free unknown. So the point can be loaded, fixed, or given a prescribed displacement, and it can be coupled onward to another point. The surface cannot deform at all: it is welded to an infinitely stiff body. How the load spreads over the surface is not prescribed; it is whatever the stiffness of the model needs to keep that surface rigid, and it piles up where the rigid patch meets the flexible material around it.

Distributing coupling (RBE3)

Here the point follows the surface. Give each coupled node a weight wi, with W=​∑wi, and measure offsets from the weighted centroid xc=​∑wixi/W: di=​xi−xc. The surface's rigid motion that fits its nodes best in the weighted least-squares sense is a translation of the centroid and a rotation:

uc=1W∑wiui,θR=H−1∑widi×ui,H=∑wi(|di|2I−didiT).

The point then moves with that rigid motion, carried from the centroid to wherever the point is:

uR=uc+θR×(xR−xc).

The point is now the dependent one: it cannot be fixed or driven, only loaded. The surface keeps all its own stiffness — the coupling adds none — so it deforms as the material around it lets it. The transpose of this map spreads a load on the point over the nodes. A force F and a moment M on the point become, at node i,

fi=wiWF+wi(H−1Mc)×di,Mc=M+(xR−xc)×F.

These nodal forces are statically equivalent to the load on the point, lever arm included. The weights decide how the force is shared. KoFEM weights each node by its share of the surface's area: the nodal shares of a uniform pressure, A/3 per corner of a linear triangle and A/3 per edge node of a quadratic one, whose corners take none. A force at the centroid is then exactly a uniform pressure on the surface, whatever the mesh. With equal weights per node it would be a uniform pressure only on a uniform mesh: a node on the edge of the surface, with half an inner node's area, would be pushed twice as hard for its area, and on quadratic faces the load would sit on the corners alone. The fit needs nodes that do not all lie on one line: a straight row of nodes cannot measure a spin about itself.

Example: a screw head on a plate

A steel plate 32 × 32 × 6 mm stands on a rigid base: its lower face is fixed. It has a clearance hole of radius 4.5 mm, as for the screw in the figure above. A screw head bears on the ring of the top face out to radius 8 mm and presses it down with 5 kN. The screw itself is not modelled: the ring is coupled to a point on the screw axis at the height of the top face, and the force is applied to that point. The ring is its own face, split off the top face as you would to pick it in the app. Netgen meshes it at 0.5 mm and the rest of the plate at 1.25 mm. The plate is solved with quadratic tetrahedra, once with each coupling.

The screw head on the plate, by coupling
Kinematic (RBE2)Distributing (RBE3)
Deflection of the point0.58 µm0.68 µm
Ring's departure from a rigid motion0 µm0.40 µm
Peak von Mises stress within r = 16 mm46 MPa34 MPa

The kinematic coupling presses the ring in as a flat, rigid punch. The plate is stiffer under it — the point moves 15.1 % less — and the stress collects where the rigid punch ends, at the ring's outer edge: 46 MPa, the highest of the two. The distributing coupling presses the ring with what amounts to a uniform pressure. The ring dishes, deepest at the hole, where the plate has no material beside it to carry the load, and the stress is highest at the hole, 34 MPa, with no peak at the ring's edge. A real head lies between the two: stiffer than the plate, but not rigid, and in contact rather than bonded.

Cut each model through the screw axis and look square on to the cut, as in the drawing above, to see the stress inside the plate. Both use one colour scale. With the kinematic coupling the stress peaks at the ring's outer edge, where the rigid ring meets the free top face. With the distributing coupling it is highest at the hole.

Example: a coupled bar end

The textbook comparison couples the free end face of a cantilever to a point at its centre. A steel bar 20 × 20 mm in section and 200 mm long is clamped at its root, meshed at 4 mm and solved with quadratic tetrahedra. Its tip point carries either a transverse force of 1 kN or a torque of 100 N·m.

The cantilever's tip, by coupling, against beam and torsion theory
Kinematic (RBE2)Distributing (RBE3)Theory
Tip deflection under the force0.9524 mm0.9525 mm0.9524 mm (bending), 0.9598 mm (with shear)
Twist of the tip face10.946 mrad (−0.6 %)10.998 mrad (−0.1 %)11.007 mrad (Saint-Venant)
Twist 20 mm from the tip9.862 mrad9.862 mrad

Under the force the two couplings give the same deflection, within 0.00 %: the tip face of a bent bar barely deforms, so holding it rigid changes almost nothing. The clamped root, which also stops the section's lateral contraction, keeps both a little below the beam value with shear.

Torsion separates them. A twisted square section warps out of its plane. The kinematic coupling forbids that at the tip, which stiffens the bar slightly; the distributing coupling allows it, but feeds the torque in as nodal forces that grow with the distance from the axis, so the corners take the most — where Saint-Venant's shear stress is actually zero. One section depth from the tip both bars twist alike, and along the bar their largest stresses lie on one curve:

In the last slice the distributing coupling reaches 98 MPa and the kinematic one 82 MPa; along the middle of the bar both read 93 MPa. This is Saint-Venant's principle: two statically equivalent loads differ only within about one section depth of where they act.

Which coupling to use

  • Distributing when the part behind the point is not much stiffer than the surface, or when only the load matters and the surface should keep its own stiffness: a remote force or moment, a bracket's load introduction, a light clamp.
  • Kinematic when the part really is much stiffer — a hardened shaft in a bore, a thick end plate — or when the point must be fixed or driven, which a distributing point cannot be.
  • Either way, read stresses only some way from the coupled surface. Within about one characteristic size of it — the head diameter, the section depth — the coupling decides the stress, not the part it stands for. If the stress right there matters, model the part and its contact instead.

KoFEM implementation

A coupling is added in Supports & Loads on picked faces, as Distributing (RBE3) or Kinematic (RBE2). The point starts at the centre of the selection; for a cylindrical selection the ends of its axis are offered too, and any position can be typed in (reference point options). The solve weights each coupled node of a distributing coupling by its share of the picked faces' area (area shares), on a quadratic mesh on the faces' edge nodes (surface couplings).

The Rust solver writes each coupling as dependent degrees of freedom: kinematic couplings make the coupled nodes dependent; distributing couplings make the point dependent, fitted about the centroid and carried to the point as above (the carry). A distributing coupling whose nodes lie on one line is refused when its point has rotations; a gap tie's node, which only needs its translation, follows the part of the motion such a row can measure (rotation flags). The coupled solid solve then reduces the system as described in solid–shell coupling.

Ties across a gap use distributing couplings too: each node of one surface is the point of a coupling to the patch of the other surface around it (tie couplings), with equal weights: the patch is a few nearby nodes, not a surface. Its node is never at the patch's centroid, so carrying the fitted motion to the node, rotation included, is what makes such a tie transmit bending.

The examples above were built from STEP files and meshed by the app's engine, then solved with the same coupled solve the app runs.

Limitations

  • Both couplings are bonded and linear: no contact, no gap opening, no friction, no preload. A screw's clamping force is a load on the point here, not a bolt pretension.
  • A distributing coupling weights its nodes by the area of the whole faces it grips. A picked node that belongs to no such face carries no area and takes no load; a selection of nodes with no whole face between them is refused.
  • Stresses within about one characteristic size of the coupled surface reflect the coupling, not the part it replaces.
  • A distributing point can be loaded but not supported; support a kinematic point instead.
