Why does a multigrid preconditioner keep the iteration count low, and how does KoFEM build one?

KoFEM solves a solid model with conjugate gradients (CG). The linear-solve page explains CG, its residual and its stopping test. This page explains the default preconditioner inside CG: smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid (AMG). Multigrid is not a separate solver here. It is applied once per CG iteration to turn a residual into a correction. The page covers static, small-strain, linear-elastic solids, as on the linear-solve page; units are N, mm and N/mm.

Why local sweeps stall

Write the constrained system as Ax=​b, with the stiffness matrix A after constraint elimination. For an estimate xk, the error ek=​x−xk and the residual rk=​b−Axk are related by Aek=​rk.

A Gauss–Seidel sweep visits each unknown in turn and sets it so that its own row balances, using the current values of its neighbours. It only sees one node and its neighbours at a time. Error that changes sign from node to node creates large local force imbalances, and a sweep removes it quickly. Error that varies slowly over many elements creates almost no imbalance at any one node, so a sweep barely changes it.

In a stiffness matrix, smooth error is soft. Its strain energy eTAe is small compared with its size, and the softest error of all is a rigid-body motion, which costs no energy where the supports allow it. A finer mesh has more nodes across the same smooth shape, so a sweep reaches less of it and the iteration count of Gauss–Seidel-preconditioned CG grows. Thin walls make it worse: bending a thin wall is soft as well.

Coarse-level correction

Smooth error can be described with far fewer unknowns than the mesh has. Multigrid builds a prolongator P, a tall sparse matrix whose columns are coarse basis vectors: each is a displacement pattern on the fine mesh. A coarse vector y stands for the fine displacement Py. The coarse matrix is the Galerkin product

Ac=PTAP,

the stiffness of the coarse basis vectors against each other. Given a residual r of an estimate x, the coarse-level correction gives

x′=x+PAc−1PTr.

PTr restricts the residual to the coarse unknowns, Ac−1 solves the small system, and P brings the answer back. Among all corrections of the form Py, this one leaves the smallest error in the energy norm eTAe: the remaining error is A-orthogonal to every coarse basis vector. So whatever smooth error the columns of P can represent, the correction removes. The smoother removes the rest. The two are complementary, and the method stands or falls with the choice of P.

Geometric multigrid takes P from a coarser mesh. KoFEM’s meshes are unstructured and have no coarser parent mesh, so P is built from the matrix and the node coordinates alone: that makes it algebraic. Applied recursively to Ac, it gives a hierarchy of levels.

Aggregates from strong couplings

Smoothed aggregation first groups neighbouring nodes into aggregates; each aggregate becomes one coarse node. Nodes are grouped along strong couplings. The coupling of nodes i and j is the 3×3 block Aij of the matrix; it counts as strong when

|Aij|F>θ|Aii|F|Ajj|F,

with Frobenius norms |·|F (the square root of the sum of squared entries) and a threshold θ. A small threshold keeps nearly every mesh neighbour; it drops couplings that are weak compared with the stiffness of the two nodes, for example across a soft material next to a stiff one.

Aggregation then runs in three passes. First, a node whose strong neighbours are all still free forms an aggregate with them. Second, each node left over joins the aggregate of its strongest neighbour. Third, any node still left forms a new aggregate with its free neighbours. Constrained nodes join none: the coarse levels must not move them. A node with no strong coupling also joins none; the smoother handles it.

Rigid-body modes on every level

Each aggregate needs coarse basis vectors that can represent the soft error on it. For an elastic solid the softest motions are the six rigid-body modes: three translations and three rotations. At a node with position (px,py,pz), measured from the centroid of the model and divided by the root-mean-square node distance from it, the modes take the values

Bnode=[1000pz−py010−pz0px001py−px0]

Rows are the node’s x, y and z displacements; columns are the three translations and the rotations about x, y and z. The scaling gives translations and rotations similar magnitudes. Rows of constrained unknowns are set to zero.

Stack these rows over the nodes of one aggregate into Bagg and factor it, Bagg=​QR, with orthonormal columns in Q. Those six columns are the aggregate’s columns of the tentative prolongator P0, zero outside the aggregate. By construction P0 reproduces every rigid-body motion exactly, on every aggregate at once: a rigid motion of the whole body is a combination of its columns. R records the modes in the coarse unknowns, so the next level repeats the process with six unknowns per coarse node. A mode an aggregate cannot carry, for example a rotation of an aggregate with too few free unknowns, is dropped as linearly dependent.

Smoothing the prolongator

P0 is piecewise rigid: each column moves one aggregate and nothing else, with a jump at the aggregate’s edge. Such jumps cost strain energy, so these basis vectors are too stiff to represent smooth error well. One damped Jacobi step on each column smooths them:

P=(I−ωD−1A)P0,ω=43ρ(D−1A).

D is the diagonal of A and ρ(D−1A) the largest eigenvalue of D−1A. The step lowers the energy of each column where it is highest, at the aggregate edges, and spreads it one layer of nodes into the neighbouring aggregates. The columns still add up to a rigid motion v of the whole body wherever Av=​0, that is away from the supports, because the step leaves such a motion unchanged. The weight ω is the usual choice of Vaněk, Mandel and Brezina; the price is a denser P and therefore a denser coarse matrix.

The V-cycle inside CG

One application of the preconditioner, z≈​A−1r, is one V-cycle through the hierarchy, starting from z=​0:

  1. On each level going down: one forward Gauss–Seidel sweep, then restrict the remaining residual to the next level with PT.
  2. On the coarsest level: solve exactly by dense Cholesky factorization.
  3. On each level going up: add the prolongated correction, then one backward Gauss–Seidel sweep.

CG needs a symmetric positive-definite preconditioner. The backward sweep is the transpose of the forward sweep, so the cycle is symmetric; the Galerkin coarse matrices are positive definite whenever A is, and with an exact coarsest solve and Gauss–Seidel sweeps the whole cycle is positive definite. This is why the cycle smooths forward on the way down and backward on the way up rather than forward both times.

Each level has a fraction of the unknowns of the one above, so a cycle costs a few fine-level matrix–vector products. The operator complexity, the nonzeros of all levels divided by those of A, measures the extra memory and work per cycle; in the study below it stays near 1.4.

Worked example: one coarse level for a bar

Take a bar of seven springs of stiffness 1 N/mm between eight nodes, fixed at both ends, nodes 0 and 7. The six free nodes give the tridiagonal matrix A with 2 on the diagonal and −1 beside it. Along a bar the only rigid motion is an axial translation. Group nodes 1–3 and 4–6 into two aggregates. The tentative prolongator has one column per aggregate, one on its nodes and zero elsewhere:

P0T=[111000000111]

KoFEM normalizes the columns, here by 1/3; scaling a column rescales its coarse unknown and leaves the correction unchanged, so this example keeps whole numbers. Every diagonal entry is 2 and the off-diagonal entries of a row add up to at most 2 in absolute value, so by Gershgorin’s theorem ρ(D−1A)≤​2. With this bound, ω=​2/3 and ωD−1=​I/3; KoFEM estimates ρ instead. Then I−A/3 has 1/3 on its three central diagonals, and each smoothed column averages three neighbouring entries of P0:

PT=[2/312/31/300001/32/312/3]

The coarse matrices follow from PTAP:

P0TAP0=[2−1−12]
PTAP=[8/9−1/3−1/38/9]

The tentative coarse matrix is the bar seen as two rigid blocks: moving one block by 1 mm stretches the spring to its support and the spring between the blocks, 2 N/mm. The smoothed basis vectors taper gradually instead of jumping, so they are much softer, 8/9 instead of 2 N/mm: they are closer to the soft, smooth error the coarse level has to remove.

Now load every free node with 1 N. The exact displacements are (3,5,6,6,5,3) mm. Start from zero and apply each preconditioner once to this load. One symmetric Gauss–Seidel sweep pair leaves 0.696 of the initial error in the energy norm. One two-level cycle, a forward sweep, the coarse correction with the smoothed P and a backward sweep, leaves 0.159. On six unknowns the difference is modest; it grows with the mesh, as the next section shows.

Iterations under mesh refinement

The point of multigrid is that its iteration count hardly depends on the mesh size. The table and figure below show CG on a plane-stress cantilever plate, four times as long as it is deep, clamped at one end and loaded at the other. The plate is cut into squares of two linear triangles each, and every refinement halves the element size. Both preconditioners stop at the same relative residual of 10−6. The multigrid follows the steps above in two dimensions, where a node has two unknowns and a body three rigid-body modes, and coarsens down to 60 unknowns so that even the smaller meshes have more than one level.

Each Gauss–Seidel iteration is cheaper than a V-cycle, but not by enough to make up for the iteration count on large models. Multigrid also costs a setup: aggregation, the prolongators and the coarse matrices are built once before CG starts.

When multigrid struggles or fails

  • Missing supports. The coarse levels carry the rigid-body modes on purpose. If the supports leave one of them free, the coarsest matrix is singular, its Cholesky factorization fails, and the solve stops before CG starts: the coarsest multigrid matrix “is not positive definite — the model is probably not fully constrained”. See insufficient constraints. Gauss–Seidel builds no coarse level, so it may still run on such a model, but the free motion stays undetermined.
  • Soft motions that are not rigid. The coarse space holds rigid motions only. Bending a thin wall is soft too, but it is not a rigid motion of an aggregate, so thin-walled parts, above all with quadratic elements, can still need many iterations.
  • Material contrast. A very stiff body next to a very soft one leaves couplings that the strength threshold may keep or drop. Large contrasts can raise the iteration count with either preconditioner.
  • Memory and setup. The coarse levels add memory in proportion to the operator complexity, and the setup takes time before the first iteration. The solve log lists the size of every level.
  • Coarsening that stops early. If a level cannot be coarsened further while still large, the coarsest level is too big to factor densely and is smoothed with a few symmetric Gauss–Seidel sweeps instead of solved. The cycle stays a valid preconditioner, but converges more slowly.

None of these makes a converged answer wrong. Multigrid changes how fast CG reaches the stopping test, not which system it solves: both preconditioners stop at the same residual, and the accuracy questions of the linear-solve page apply unchanged.

KoFEM implementation

The multigrid is part of KoFEM’s Rust finite-element module and is selected as “AMG multigrid” under “Solver” in the Solve panel, the default for solid models.

Multigrid settings in KoFEM
SettingValue
Strength threshold θ0.02 for solid solves; 0 in topology optimization
Near-null spaceSix rigid-body modes per node, from the node coordinates
Prolongator smoothingOne damped Jacobi step, ω=​4/(3ρ), ρ from 15 power iterations raised by 5 %
Stop coarseningAt 600 unknowns or less, after 10 levels, or when a level no longer shrinks
SmootherForward Gauss–Seidel down, backward Gauss–Seidel up, one sweep each
Coarsest levelDense Cholesky up to 2000 unknowns; otherwise four symmetric Gauss–Seidel sweep pairs
  • The solid solve builds the hierarchy after eliminating the constraints and marks the constrained unknowns; the setup repeats aggregation and prolongator construction level by level.
  • Strong couplings, the three-phase aggregation, the rigid-body modes, the tentative prolongator, its smoothing and the Galerkin product implement the steps above. A coarse unknown whose mode was dropped keeps a unit diagonal and a zero right-hand side.
  • The V-cycle is applied by preconditioned CG. amg.ts writes the unknowns of every level to the solve log, and a singular coarsest level raises the setup error.
  • In topology optimization the stiffness changes with the densities each design. A threshold of 0 makes the aggregates follow the mesh rather than the densities, so the SIMP solve keeps the coarse levels for three designs, swapping in only the new fine matrix, before rebuilding them.
  • In a solve with couplings, a coupling’s reference point carries rotations instead of translations, and its modes are set accordingly. Pure shell models do not use multigrid; they solve with incomplete Cholesky preconditioning.

KoFEM’s automated tests compare multigrid solves of real models with stored reference results and cover the setup and the V-cycle directly. The worked example and the refinement study on this page are separate calculations that illustrate the method; they do not run KoFEM’s code.

Why KoFEM uses smoothed aggregation

Choice and rationale. Smoothed-aggregation AMG with the rigid-body modes as coarse basis is a standard preconditioner for elasticity. It needs only the matrix and the node coordinates, so it works on Netgen’s unstructured tetrahedral meshes and on hexahedral meshes alike. It keeps CG iteration counts low as models grow, where Gauss–Seidel’s grow with the mesh, and it fits in the browser’s memory because the coarse levels add only a fraction of the matrix.

Alternatives. Geometric multigrid needs nested meshes, which an unstructured mesher does not produce. Classical (Ruge–Stüben) AMG was designed for scalar problems and needs extensions to see rigid rotations. Incomplete Cholesky, which KoFEM uses for shells, has no coarse level, so its iterations also grow with the mesh. A sparse direct factorization avoids iterations but its fill-in costs memory that large models do not have in the browser. Gauss–Seidel stays selectable as the simple choice.

Tradeoffs and limits. Multigrid costs a setup step and memory for its levels, fails early on under-constrained models, and helps less where soft motions are not rigid, such as thin walls with quadratic elements. A coarse space for those motions, a p-multigrid step from quadratic to linear elements or a stronger smoother would be the next things to try there.
